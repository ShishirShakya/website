There are frequent times I have been asked to review a Ph.D. candidate's research. They often ask how I became so productive. They ask for the recipe. They ask how to be a leader.

I sit on that side of a gate when I review. I do not recognize myself as a leader. I have seen the cost of handing that title out too soon.

I am a fortunate follower of many kinder and generous people, people to whom I have only weak ties.

Over many years I have looked carefully at how I think. This essay is my way of saying it.

## The Uneven Map

The world advances through a complex interplay of intentionality, agency, context, and, at times, randomness.

While access and privilege undoubtedly shape opportunities, other factors also play significant roles in determining outcomes. Those factors include individual effort, chance, and evolving societal norms. For example, research from the Pew Charitable Trusts and Raj Chetty's studies on social mobility demonstrate that both structural factors and individual choices contribute to opportunity. Upward mobility is possible, though unevenly distributed.

Like many, I was not handed a map. I learned in pieces, from books and leftover notes, but I tried to stay ready. Luck still mattered. A conversation I had not planned opened a door I could not have scheduled. The work I had already done let me walk through it.

That is not a theory of fairness. Plenty of people prepare just as carefully and never get the chance. A success story can teach something. You cannot infer from one winner, or a few winners, that everyone had an equal shot.

Survivors are visible. The people who prepared and never got the opening are not. Using the winner as the test confuses a possible path with an even playing field.

This essay is based on personal memoir. It is for anyone navigating systems: policy leaders, educators, young professionals, and anyone who has ever felt the rules were unwritten or the gate was locked. From the uneven map, I learned not just to walk, but how to navigate. Navigation requires principles. Those principles shape everything that follows.

Many of us are not taught how to navigate the unwritten rules that influence opportunity. That is sometimes due to systemic barriers, sometimes due to circumstance, and sometimes simply by chance. These barriers are not distributed evenly. Systemic exclusion persists even when effort and preparation are present.

I recognize that not everyone starts with the same resources or opportunities. That's why my unfinished map may still help someone else begin their path. The map is shaped by my own context and limitations. It may inspire them to chart their own.

At the same time, some people have used the structures that were available to them and moved up. That shows more than one path is possible, even when the system is imperfect.

## Principles of Navigation

### Who I am

I am an economist by training, a teacher by nature, and a storyteller by necessity. I believe knowledge is most valuable when it empowers rather than excludes. I also recognize the need for standards, rigor, and sometimes selectivity to ensure quality, safety, and coherence.

### Chesterton's Fence

Chesterton's Fence is one of my guiding principles. Before I challenge a system, I try to reconstruct the logic that built it. I cannot always interview the people who wrote the rules of play. I can still ask what failure the rule was built to stop, who still defends it, and what would break if it vanished overnight. Change should be principled rather than merely symbolic.

Principled change is guided by clear criteria. It weighs evidence. It considers the perspectives of both beneficiaries and those defending the status quo. It seeks to mitigate harm while pursuing improvement.

### Structure and openness

I want to learn and teach what makes life worth living. I want to understand how people flourish, think freely, and seek truth. I value autonomy and decentralization. I also recognize the importance of structure, shared norms, and collective wisdom for progress.

However, history and recent experience show that excessive openness, without sufficient structure, can lead to negative outcomes. One example is the spread of misinformation on open online platforms. Another is the erosion of educational standards through unchecked credential inflation.

I hope that the paths I've walked stay open for others. I hope they inspire others to redraw new ones. I still stumble, revise, and unlearn. Even an imperfect map can help someone take a clearer first step.

I am seeking visibility, not for self-promotion. I want to amplify alignment and promote unity. I want to be seen without compromising my values. I want to tell truer stories, not louder ones. Those stories should awaken curiosity and clarify meaning.

### Stochastic tinkering

For every abstract principle, I strive to illustrate it with a concrete example. When I refer to "stochastic tinkering." I mean many small tries, low downside, and room for luck. I look for experiments where failure will not compromise livelihoods or erode trust. For instance, before rolling out a new course, I pilot it with a handful of volunteers.

I'm learning to go deeper, not wider. But I don't have an ecosystem that naturally supports depth. My strategy has been adaptive. I cast a wide net. I offer value and kindness. I stay ready for when the big opportunity arises.

Honoring complexity means acknowledging trade-offs. It means acknowledging the limitations of one's own perspective. It means acknowledging the possibility of unintended consequences.

## The Gate and the Game

### False negatives and false positives

In the above navigation principle, there is pain in being a false negative, overlooked despite merit. Capable, prepared, but still invisible in elite spaces. 

Systems for recognizing merit must also prevent false positives, recognizing individuals without readiness. Balancing these errors is a complex challenge, with real stakes for both individuals and society.

Academic peer review and professional licensure are forms of necessary gatekeeping that protect quality and safety. They also risk overlooking unconventional talent. This is not merely an individual burden but a collective moral responsibility. It's about how we, as a society, decide who gets noticed and who doesn't. It's about who gets the chance to contribute and who remains unseen.

Both types of error, false positives and negatives, shape lives and institutions. The "gate" and "game" metaphors help us see the stakes clearly.

I've lived the tension of not knowing what to say "no" to. I have been productive but scattered. I have tried to be discoverable to many, and in doing so, weakened my gravitational pull for the right few.

Still, I hold this tension carefully. Because I know the cost of a false positive, recognized without readiness, in decision-making spaces can be even greater.

The consequences of false positives are illustrated in well-known cases. Failed leadership appointments have occurred where unprepared or unethical leaders caused harm to organizations and the public. Policy errors have come from the premature promotion of ideas that have not yet been tested or proven. Their choices ripple outward, often at the expense of those who never got the chance.

## The Path Forward

My direction is to play the game with clarity. I aim to master the gate, not to guard it, but to open it and leave it open.

My goal is to build what I wish existed. I want to tell stories that bring others in. I want to stay principled, even when the rewards are slow to come.

Systems don't just emerge. They persist, not always because they work. Sometimes they persist because of inertia, coordination problems, or because someone benefits from the friction. For example, the QWERTY keyboard layout persists not because it is optimal, but due to coordination and switching costs.

Part of principled change is understanding incentives and trade-offs, not just the stated ideals.

I recognize that not all change is desirable or feasible. Humility and ongoing dialogue are essential for real progress. That dialogue must cross different cultures, histories, and organizational contexts. It is essential for reassessing assumptions as new evidence emerges.

Because the door I open isn't just for me. It's for the next person who arrives without a map. If you remember only one thing, let it be this. Principled change means opening the door and questioning the map, so that those who follow can go farther than we ever imagined.
